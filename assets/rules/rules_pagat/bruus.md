# Bruus

**Nombre de cartes :** 52
**Difficulté :** —
**Type :** Karnöffel, Plis
**But :** A four player card game from North Friesland, Germany, a descendant of the medieval game Karnöffel

---

## Introduction

Bruus is a north German game which was probably invented over 300 years ago. Called Brusbart until the 20th century, it spawned a family of similar games that spread out all over northern Europe as far as Russia, Scandinavia and the Baltic states. By the middle of the 18th century, it was reported as being played by middle class women and the lesser nobility in Poland, by the German population of Baltic Livonia and by the peasantry in northern Germany, where a character in a 1781 play written in the Low German dialect declares that *“my leevtes Spill is Bruusbaart!” * (“my favourite game is Brusbart!”). Brusbart probably reached its zenith around 1800 when it was Hamburg’s national game and was even the subject of a song which suggests that signalling between partners was an accepted part of, what was then, quite a boisterous and rowdy game.

Although signalling is no longer part of the game, it is one of several clues that point to its descent from Europe’s oldest card game, Karnöffel. These clues include the wildly disturbed ranking of its cards and the unusual role of the Sevens. There are, in fact, three families of games descended from Karnöffel which may be distinguished by the number of their chosen suits. These act like trump suits but contain cards with powers that vary from unbeatable to none. Looked at from that perspective, games of the Brusbart family have four chosen suits – every suit has cards with full or partial powers and cards which are effectively useless.

The known survivors of the family with four chosen suits, apart from the German game of Bruus described on this page, include Danish Brus, Gothlandic Bräus, [Icelandic Brús](https://is.wikipedia.org/wiki/Br%C3%BAs) in the valley of Svarfaðardalurand and the Greenlandic game Voormsi. Since the latter half of the 19th century, German Bruus appears to have been restricted to the Anglian peninsula and neighbouring Central Schleswig. Today, it is only played in a handful of villages in the hinterland of the fishing port of Husum, tournaments being held in Ahrenviöl, Immenstedt, Wester- and Oster-Ohrstedt and Schwesing. The “Frohsinn” male voice choir in Treia have played Bruus after choir practice since their foundation in 1881.

The rules have changed surprisingly little since then; terminology has changed, double and triple tricks have been added and there are slight local variations. The version given here is the game as played in Schwesing. 

*This page is based on a contribution from Paul Eaton whose research included a field trip to North Friesland where he learned the game from members of the male voice choir “Frohsinn” in Treia at their traditional post-choir Bruus evening on Monday 2 March 2020, and from Bruus teacher, Dietrich Schoon, before participating in a local tournament in Schwesing on Tuesday 3 March 2020.*

## Players and Objective

Four people play, two against two in partnerships. Partners sit opposite one another. There are 36 cards and nine tricks. The team which takes five tricks wins the hand. Won hands are scored using matchsticks. Game is 12 matchsticks.

## The Cards and their Ranking

![Bruus Top Trumps](../images/karnoeff/BruusTopTrumps.png)
A 36-card, Berlin-pattern, French-suited pack is used, consisting of A-K-Q(D)-J(B)-10-9-8-7-6 in each of the suits clubs, spades, hearts and diamonds. Packs sold online for the games of Jass and Tapp are ideal, but these do not seem to be sold in north Germany, so they remove the fives, fours, threes, and twos from a standard 52-card Rommé or Poker pack. The suits rank from high to low clubs, spades, hearts, diamonds, as in Skat.

There are 3 matadors (*oberste Trümpfe*), in order from highest to lowest: the ![club](../images/internat/club.gif)J, known as *Spitz*; the ![heart](../images/internat/heart.gif)K, known as *Bruus*; and the ![spade](../images/internat/spade.gif)8, known as the *Toller Hund* (“mad dog”).

Next come the ‘beaters’ (*Stecher *or *Stekers*) which beat one another in rank and then suit order from highest to lowest as follows:

These 18 cards, the matadors and beaters, are collectively known as 'counters'.

The four Sevens have a special role. They are unbeatable when led to a trick except by a Seven of a higher suit, but when played to a trick in progress they cannot beat anything except a Seven of a lower suit. Thus for example the ![spade](../images/internat/spade.gif)7 can be beaten by the ![club](../images/internat/club.gif)7 and it can beat the two red Sevens. When led it cannot be beaten by any card other than the ![club](../images/internat/club.gif)7, and when played to a trick it cannot win unless one of the red Sevens was led to the trick.

The other 14 cards - the remaining Kings and Eights and all the Queens and Tens - are ‘duds’ (*Luschen, Schietkarten* or simply *Schiet*) and cannot beat any other cards, even those of the same rank or suit. 

## The Deal** **

Deal and play are clockwise. The dealer shuffles the pack and offers it to the player to the right. There is then a ritual for cutting as follows: the cutter cuts the pack, examines the lowest card and shows it to an opponent; the dealer then completes the cut and examines the lowest card, showing it to an opponent. If either card turns out to be a matador, the pack is reshuffled. Once this procedure is over, the dealer deals three cards to each player in one packet and in turn beginning with forehand (the player to dealer's left). The remainder are placed face down to form the *bunk* (stock).

## The Play

Forehand leads any card to the first trick, followed by the remaining players in turn. There is never any obligation to follow suit: players may always play any card to any trick. 

- If a Seven is led, the trick is taken by the player of the highest Seven.

- If any card other than a Seven is led, the trick is taken by the highest counter (i.e. matador or beater) if any are played. 

- If a dud is led and no counters are played to the trick, the card that was led wins the trick.

The trick winner draws the top card from the stock; the others do likewise in turn. The trick winner then leads to the next trick. One player from each partnership collects the tricks for their team. As in any plain trick game it is usual to lay tricks out in a row, alternately ‘in line’ and ‘crosswise’ so that everyone can see how many have been taken.

As soon as a team has one five or more tricks the play ends immediately and the deal is scored.

If the stock is exhausted, play continues in the same way, but without drawing, until one team has at least five tricks.

### Double and Triple Tricks

A player on lead who has two cards of the same nominal rank may play both at once. In order to beat this lead, a subsequent player must beat each card separately, beating only one does not count. The beating cards do not have to be of the same rank. Another player may beat the two cards that are currently winning the trick by beating both of them, and so on. So if a player leads two duds, for example two Queens, another player requires two beaters, for example a Six and a Jack, to beat the Queens and win the double trick provided these cards are not in turn beaten by others.

Two cards may be of the same nominal rank even though one is a matador. For example a player could lead ![heart](../images/internat/heart.gif)K-![diamond](../images/internat/diamond.gif)K to start a double trick, and this could only be beaten by the ![club](../images/internat/club.gif)J accompanied by a counter.

**Example A.** Player West leads ![diamond](../images/internat/diamond.gif)10-![spade](../images/internat/spade.gif)10, North beats this with ![heart](../images/internat/heart.gif)6-![diamond](../images/internat/diamond.gif)A, and East beats North with ![heart](../images/internat/heart.gif)J-![club](../images/internat/club.gif)9. Suppose that South's cards are ![spade](../images/internat/spade.gif)9, ![spade](../images/internat/spade.gif)A and ![heart](../images/internat/heart.gif)A. These cards cannot be used to win the trick because neither of them beats the ![club](../images/internat/club.gif)9, so South plays the two Aces (South's lowest cards) and East wins the double trick. 

**Example B**. Suppose we exchange the card holdings of East and South. West leads ![diamond](../images/internat/diamond.gif)10-![spade](../images/internat/spade.gif)10, North beats this with ![heart](../images/internat/heart.gif)6-![diamond](../images/internat/diamond.gif)A and East plays ![spade](../images/internat/spade.gif)A-![heart](../images/internat/heart.gif)A. Suppose South's cards are ![club](../images/internat/club.gif)9-![heart](../images/internat/heart.gif)J-![club](../images/internat/club.gif)6. South cannot win this trick because only one of South's cards is good enough to beat the Aces. So South plays the Jack and Six and East wins again.

These examples illustrate that the winner of a double trick is determined not only by the ranks of the cards played to the trick, but sometimes also by the order in which they are played.

A player who holds three cards of the same rank may lead them immediately regardless of whose turn it would normally be to lead to the trick. The equal cards that are led will win the triple trick unless another player beats all three separately, but not necessarily with cards of equal rank. If two players have three cards, positional priority applies. For example, East wins a trick and is on lead, but North has the ![spade](../images/internat/spade.gif)A, ![heart](../images/internat/heart.gif)A and ![diamond](../images/internat/diamond.gif)A, North can take over the lead and play them. They can only be beaten by three higher cards such as ![club](../images/internat/club.gif)A, ![diamond](../images/internat/diamond.gif)9 and ![club](../images/internat/club.gif)J. But if West also has 3 nominally equal cards, such as ![club](../images/internat/club.gif)8, ![spade](../images/internat/spade.gif)8 and ![heart](../images/internat/heart.gif)8, West can claim positional priority (since it was East's lead, in the normal sequence of play West would have been the third player to the trick while North was the fourth player) and lead the Eights. North's three Aces cannot beat them, as none of them beats the *Toller Hund* even though, frustratingly for North, any of the Aces could have beaten the two dud Eights. 

A double or triple trick counts as two or three tricks respectively for the team that wins it. The winner draws the next 2 or 3 cards, respectively, from the *bunk *in one go, followed by the other players in turn. If there are not enough cards in the *bunk* for all players to fully replenish their hands, players only take their share of the *bunk*.

### Daring and Striking

Daring and striking (*wagen und schlagen*) are a key feature of the game. Playing the second or third highest matador (*Bruus* or *Toller Hund*) counts as a dare if the following conditions are all met.

1. The next higher card has not yet been played, and

2. the darer does not hold the next higher card, and

3. the darer is not the last player to the trick, and

4. the *bunk* has not been exhausted.

A strike occurs if the next higher card is played to the trick by an opponent of the darer: in that case the striking team scores 2 points. If there is no strike, the dare succeeds and the darer's team scores 1 point. If the *Toller Hund* is dared and then struck by the *Bruus*, the play of the *Bruus* will itself be a dare (*weiter wagen*) if the four conditions above are met. In this case if the dare with the *Bruus* succeeds the team wins 3 points, two for the strike and one for the dare, but if the *Bruus* is itself struck by an opponent's *Spitz* the team that played the *Toller Hund* and *Spitz* score 4 points for the counterstrike.

The following points should be noted.

- A dared matador can only be struck by the next higher matador. The *Spitz* cannot strike the *Toller Hund*.

- A dared card cannot be struck by the darer's partner, only by an opponent.

- It is possible for one team to dare twice in the same trick if the leader to the trick plays the *Toller Hund* and the partner of the leader plays the *Bruus*. This will score 2 points for the two dares if the fourth player does not play the *Spitz*.

- In any trick, only one team can score for daring and striking. In the above case where both partners dare, if the fourth player strikes with the *Spitz* the striking team will score 3 points for the two dares and the strike.

- It is not necessary for the daring or striking cards to win the trick for the dare or strike to score. Daring and striking can occur in a trick to which a Seven is led, when none of the matadors have any power, or during a double or triple trick if the relevant matadors are played and the conditions met.

- After a successful dare, the darer must briefly show their remaining card or cards to an opponent. It suffices if the opponent can see the next higher card is not held.

- North: ![diamond](../images/internat/diamond.gif)7, East ![spade](../images/internat/spade.gif)8, South ![heart](../images/internat/heart.gif)K, West ![club](../images/internat/club.gif)J. North wins the trick but East-West score 4 points for a counterstrike.

## Scoring

At the start of the game one player is nominated as the scorer (*Schreiber*) and given a box or tray of matches (typically one half of the plastic box in which the cards came). For each point scored, one matchstick is awarded. Points are scores as follows:

- For the team that wins five or more tricks – **1 point**

- For a team that wins five tricks when their opponents have not won any – **2 points** instead of 1.

- Successful dare of ![spade](../images/internat/spade.gif)8 or ![heart](../images/internat/heart.gif)K – **1 point**.

- Successful strike of ![spade](../images/internat/spade.gif)8 by ![heart](../images/internat/heart.gif)K or of ![heart](../images/internat/heart.gif)K by ![club](../images/internat/club.gif)J – **2 points**

- Successful strike of ![spade](../images/internat/spade.gif)8 by ![heart](../images/internat/heart.gif)K and successful onward dare of ![heart](../images/internat/heart.gif)K – **3 points**

- Successful counterstrike (*Generalschlag*) of ![heart](../images/internat/heart.gif)K by ![club](../images/internat/club.gif)J after ![heart](../images/internat/heart.gif)K has been dared onwards – **4 points** 

- Successful dare of both ![spade](../images/internat/spade.gif)8 and ![heart](../images/internat/heart.gif)K – **2 points**

- Successful strike of ![heart](../images/internat/heart.gif)K by ![club](../images/internat/club.gif)J when the opponents have dared ![spade](../images/internat/spade.gif)8 and ![heart](../images/internat/heart.gif)K – **3 points**

The winners are the first team to collect 12 or more points (matchsticks). This can occur during the play for daring and striking or at the end of the play for having won five tricks. If there is a dare or strike in the final trick, the points for daring and striking are awarded before the points for the team that took five tricks.

## Variants

### Treia Variant

The male voice choir in Treia play to slightly different rules. The main differences are:

- A 32-card Skat pack is used. There are therefore no Sixes and only 8 tricks.

- This means that a draw is possible if each team takes 4 tricks. This is a *Bock* and the point is carried forward to the next deal.

- Game is ten matches, not twelve. Each team starts with ten and discards one for each point won. If a team discards 10 matches while their opponents have either not discarded any or only discarded matches for daring and striking, the game is worth double.

- In addition to the cutting ritual, there are others associated with keeping the score properly, the infringement of which costs a round of schnapps.

### Tournament Scoring

In the Schwesing tournament, players played three sessions of four rounds, each round comprising four deals, and players were randomly paired off in each session. Points won by a team were credited to both players so that each player ended up with an individual score at the end to determine the ranking. For example, suppose that a player scores 18 matches in the first session with one partner, the same player with a different partner scores 23 matches in the second session and then teams up with a 3rd partner for the final session, scoring 33 matches. The player's total score for the evening would be 18 + 23 + 33 = 74.

### Two- and Six-Player Bruus

Two of Paul Eaton's hosts told him that they played two-hand Bruus at home with their wives. One said they played to win nine of sixteen tricks and the other said they played to win five tricks first. Otherwise the rules are the same, except that a counterstrike is not possible.  The Schwesing rules mention that Bruus can be played by six players in two teams of three with modifications, but do not state what these modifications are.

### Daring

In the modern game, playing the second or third highest matador is automatically a dare if the conditions are met. However, it is clear from some historical accounts that in the past daring was optional. If the four conditions were met, a player playing the* Toller Hund* or* Bruus* had the choice to dare (scoring 1 or 2 points respectively if successful but losing the same amount in case of a strike) or not dare (neither scoring nor risking any points). For example, near the end of a game with each team needing just one point to win, a player who was confident that their team could take five tricks would not dare when playing a *Toller* or *Bruus; *instead of risking a strike that would lose the game it would be better to wait for the more certain point for taking five tricks.
